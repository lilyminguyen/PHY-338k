\section{Lab 7: Operational Amplifiers I}

\subsection{Open-loop op-amp, comparator}

We built the circuit in Figure VII-3 and found that it functions as an
open-loop op-amp comparator. The input voltage $V_\text{in}(t)$ is a
small-amplitude sine wave centered about zero and the output voltage
$V_\text{out}(t)$ switches sharply between the maximum and minimum values
where the input crosses zero.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{7.1a.png}
    \caption{Oscilloscope display showing the open-loop op-amp acting as a 
    comparator. CH1 is $V_\text{in}(t)$ and CH2 is $V_\text{out}(t)$.}
    \label{fig:sine_comparator}
\end{figure} 

\noindent The linear gain region of the op-amp (the slope near $V_\text{in}=0$)
is small and does not play a role in this circuit because the op-amp's
open-loop gain is very large. Even a tiny input difference would drive the
output to the supply rails, so the circuit would saturate immediately rather
than operate linearly. Thus, this op-amp acts as a comparator rather than
an amplifier.\\

\noindent We measured the output voltages to be $V_\text{OM}^+=13.9\si{\volt}$
and $V_\text{OM}^-=-13.7\si{\volt}$, which are both slightly less than the
$\pm15\si{\volt}$ supply rails. According to the LF411 datasheet, the typical
maximum output swing under a $10\si{\kilo\ohm}$ load is $\pm13.5\si{\volt}$.
Our values were consistent with the specifications, so we confirm the op-amp's
performs as expected.\\

\noindent Next, we changed the input to be a $4.32\si{\volt}$ square wave and
measured the slew rate of the op-amp using the rise time. The slew rate is the
fastest rate at which its output voltage can change.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slew rate}=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t}=\frac{27.6\si{\volt}}{2.1\si{\micro\second}}\approx13.1\si{\volt}/\si{\micro\second}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{7.1b.png}
    \caption{Oscilloscope display of the input voltage (CH1) and output 
    voltage (CH2) for an open-loop op-amp comparato with a square wave
    input.}
    \label{fig:square_comparator}
\end{figure} 

\noindent Our measured slew rate of approximately $13.1\si{\volt}/\si{\micro\second}$ 
is close to the LF411 datasheet, which gives a typical slew rate of 
$13\si{\volt}/\si{\micro\second}$.

\subsection{Inverting amplifier}

Next, we built the inverting amplifier circuit from Figure VII-4 using an LF411
op-amp. For a gain of $-10$, we used $R_\text{in}=9.16\si{\kilo\ohm}$ and 
$R_f=102.2\si{\kilo\ohm}$. For a gain of $-100$, we used $R_\text{in}=5.2\si{\kilo\ohm}$
and $R_f=556\si{\kilo\ohm}$. For both setups, we used a grounding resistor $R_g=1.088\si{\kilo\ohm}$.\\

\noindent We applied a sine wave input from $10$Hz to $1$MHz and measured the
input and output voltages on the scope to calculate the gain at each frequency.
Figure~\ref{fig:invert_amp} shows the gain vs. frequency on a log-log scale
for both setups.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{7.2.a.png}
    \caption{Plot of gain vs. frequency for resistance ratios of 10 and 100 on a log-log scale.}
    \label{fig:invert_amp}
\end{figure} 

\noindent Fromm the roll-off points, we can estimate that the open-loop gain
equals the closed-loop gain at the start of each drop. This would mean the 
open-loop gain is about $10^5$ at $30$kHz and $10^4$ at $300$kHz. From these
two points, we estimate that the open-loop gain decreases approximately to $\frac{1}{f}$,
This is consistent with a gain-bandwidth product on the order of $10^6$Hz,
which is expected for the LF411 op-amp.\\

\noindent Assuming we have an ideal-opamp (no input current, $V_+=V_-$), the 
inverting intput is at virtual ground. When we apply Ohm's law:
\begin{align}
    I_\text{in}&=\frac{V_\text{in}}{R_\text{in}}\\
    I_f&=\frac{-V_\text{out}}{R_f}\\
\end{align}
\noindent setting $I_\text{in}=I_f$:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{in}}=\frac{-R_f}{R_\text{in}}
\end{equation}
\noindent which shows that the gain depends only on the resistor ratio and
includes a $180^\circ$ phase shift.


\subsection{Summing amplifier}

Finally, we built a summing amplifier using the standard inverting amplifier
configuation where the output voltage is:
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{out}=-R_f\left(\frac{V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_2}{R_2} \right)
\end{equation}
We kept both input voltages constant at $V_1=V_2=+5\si{\volt}$ and varied the
the resistor vaues to observe out $V_\text{out}$ depends on $R_1$, $R_2$, and 
$R_f$. Our measured values are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sumamp} below.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Measured output voltage $V_\text{out}$ for various resistor combinations}
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        $R_1$ ($\si{\kilo\ohm}$) & $R_2$ ($\si{\kilo\ohm}$) & $R_f$ ($\si{\kilo\ohm}$) & $V_\text{out}$ (V)\\
        \hline
        10.0 & 10.0 & 10.0 & -8.75\\
        5.34 & 10.0 & 10.0 & -13.03\\
        20.1 & 10.0 & 10.0 & -6.75\\
        10.0 & 5.34 & 10.0 & -12.81\\
        10.0 & 20.1 & 10.0 & -6.53\\
        10.0 & 10.0 & 5.34 & -5.40\\
        10.0 & 10.0 & 20.1 & -13.55\\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \label{tab:sumamp}
\end{table}

\noindent Our results were consistent with expected behavior. For example,
increasing $R_f$ increased the magnitude of $V_\text{out}$, whereas increasing
either $R_1$ or $R_2$ decreased their respective contribution of $V_1$ or $V_2$
to the output.\\

\noindent The general formula for an inverting summing amplifier with $N$ inputs is:
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{out}=-R_f\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{V_i}{R_i},
\end{equation}
\noindent which allows weighted contributions from each input voltage based on
its resistor value.



\section{Lab 8: Operational Amplifiers II}

\subsection{Op-amp input offset}

We built an inverting amplifier with a gain $G=-1000$ from Figure VIII-2 
to measure the input offset voltage $V_\text{OS}$ of various op-amps. Since we
grounded the input of the circuit ($V_\text{in}=0$), the output voltage we
measured was entirely due to the internal input offset voltage of the op-amp.
Using the gain formula $V_\text{out}=G\cdot V_\text{in}$, we substituted
$V_\text{in}=V_\text{OS}$ to get:
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{OS}=\frac{V_\text{out}}{G}
\end{equation}
\noindent Table~\ref{tab:opamp_vos} summarizes our data and calculations.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|}
    \hline
    Op-Amp Type & Measured $V_\text{out}$ (V) & Calculated $V_\text{OS}$ (mV) \\
    \hline
     & 0.497 & 0.497 \\
    LF411 & 0.393 & 0.393 \\
     & 1.124 & 1.124 \\
    \hline
     & 0.850 & 0.850 \\
    LM741 & 0.480 & 0.480 \\
     & 1.110 & 1.110 \\
    \hline
     & 0.163 & 0.163 \\
    OPA227 & 0.145 & 0.145 \\
     & 0.147 & 0.147 \\
    \hline
     & 0.153 & 0.153 \\
    LTC1150 & 0.153 & 0.153 \\
     & 0.159 & 0.159 \\
    \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Measured output voltages and computed input offset voltages for various op-amps.}
    \label{tab:opamp_vos}
\end{table}

\noindent The LF411 op-amps had input offset voltages ranging from $0.393$mV to
$1.124$mV, which were within the datasheet's typical value of $0.8$mV and below
the maximum of $2$mV with variation among individual devices. The LM741 op-amps
had offsets between $0.480$mV and $1.110$mV, which were also within the expected
datasheet range (typical $1$mV, maximum $5$mV).\\

\noindent The OPA227 and LTC1150 are designed for extremely low offset voltages
($10$µV and $0.5$µV typical), whereas our measured values were around $0.15$mV.
This is much higher than expected and likely due to limitations in our setup,
such as thermoelectric effects or noise. Thermoelectric potentials appear at 
junctions between different metals when a temperature gradient is present and
produces small voltage differences that are amplified significantly in 
high-gain circuits such as this one.\\

\noindent Overall, our measurements align with expected values for the LF411 and
LM741, but not so much for the OPA227 and LTC1150 which had magnitudes lower expected
values for the offset input voltage.

\noindent When we removed $R_2$, the output no longer leveled off cleanly in
response to the square wave. The tops and bottoms of the waveform became 
slanted rather than flat, which indicates the op-amp continued integrating
any small DC offset. Originally, $R_2$ was included to limit DC gain
and stabilize the output, so our observations make sense.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{8.2.r2removed.png}

    \label{fig:r2_removed}

\noindent With a $10$kHz, $0.74$V square wave input, we found that closing 
the switch caused the output to shift slightly upward and the amplitude to
drop by about $0.2$V. The switch effectively resets the integrator by 
discharging the capacitor, which clamps the integrator to a baseline voltage.\\

\noindent The passive RC circuit from Lab 2 acts as a low-pass filter, but it
can behave as an integrator when the input frequency is much higher than the
circuit's cutoff frequency ($f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}$). However, since it lacks
the feedback mechanism present in the op-amp circuit, it only approximates the
integration and only works under specific conditions.\\

\noindent The op-amp integrator from this lab uses negative feedback to maintain
a virtual ground at the inverting input, which allows it to integrate more 
accurately over a wider range of frequencies, including lower ones. In contrast,
if we operate the passive RC circuit below its cutoff frequency, it would behave
more like a voltage divider and its output would not represent the integral of
the input.\\

\noindent Although both circuits have the same basic layout, they can be
represented as low-pass filters. The op-amp integrator behaves like a true
integrator when the input frequency is well below the cutoff freuqency
$f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}$. As the frequency approaches or exceeeds the cutoff,
the circuit begins to attenuate the high-frequency components rather than
integrate, so its behavior would better be described as that of a low-pass
filter.

\subsection{Battery-operated photodiode circuit}

\subsubsection{Battery-powered photodiode amplifier}

Lastly, we built the battery-powered photodiode amplifier from Figure VIII-7.
We used a feedback resistor of $R=1.17\si{\mega\ohm}$, which makes the 
transimpedance gain of this circuit:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Gain}=\frac{V_\text{out}}{I_\text{in}}=R=1.17\times 10^6\si{\ohm}
\end{equation}
\noindent We tested the circuit under four lighting conditions and recorded
the output voltage using the oscilloscope:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Light blocked: $V_\text{out}\approx0.72\si{\volt}$ (used as baseline)
    \item Room light only: $V_\text{out}\approx4.08\si{\volt}$
    \item Phone light at a distance: $V_\text{out}\approx 4.25\si{\volt}$
    \item Phone light up close: $V_\text{out}\approx4.56\si{\volt}$
\end{enumerate}
\noindent As expected, the output voltage increased with light intensity. Our
circuit remained stable and did not oscillate, so there was no need to add
additional components such as a compensating capacitor.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.32\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{8.3.blocked.png}
        \caption{Light blocked}
        \label{fig:photodiode_blocked}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.32\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{8.3.roomlight.png}
        \caption{Room light only}
        \label{fig:photodiode_roomlight}
    \end{subfigure}

    \end{subfigure}

    As expected, the output voltage increases with light intensity.}
    \label{fig:photodiode}
\end{figure}

\noindent Using the photodiode's responsivity value of approximately $0.5$A/W,
we estimated the minimum light power we can detect with our photodiode and
circuit. The difference in $V_\text{out, min}$ from the blocked light to the room 
light case was roughly:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta V=4.00-0.56=3.44\si{\volt}
\end{equation}
\noindent which corresponds to a photocurrent of:
\begin{equation}
    I=\frac{\Delta V}{R}=\frac{3.44}{1.17\times 10^6}\approx2.94\si{\micro\ampere}
\end{equation}
\noindent Using the responsivity:
\begin{equation}
    P=\frac{I}{0.5}\approx5.88\si{\micro\watt}
\end{equation}

\noindent So, the minimum light power we could detect was approximately $5.9$µW.\\

\noindent For the brightest case (phone light up close, $V_\text{out}\approx4.56$V),
assuming the baseline is still approximately $0.56$V, the change is:
\begin{align}
    \Delta V &= 4.56 - 0.56 = 4.00\si{\volt}\\
    I&=\frac{4.00}{1.17\times10^6}\approx3.42\si{\micro\ampere}\\
    P&=\frac{I}{0.5}\approx6.84\si{\micro\watt}
\end{align}

\noindent So, the maximum light power detected was approximately $6.8$µW.\\

\noindent These values are reasonable for a small silicon photodiode in a
low-power indoor setup. The amplifier's high gain enabled it to convert 
the small changes in light intensity to measurable voltage changes.
