\subsubsection{Flip-flop with feedback}

We connected the D flip-flop in a feedback configuration by wiring $\overline{Q}$
to the $D$ input. The output $Q$ toggled between 0 and 1 with each rising edge of
the clock, so we confirm the flip-flop's divide-by-2 function.\\

\noindent Using a function generator and oscilloscope, we observed that $Q$ continued to
toggle as expected. We measured a propagation delay between the clock edge and $Q$
transition as approximately $t_\text{prop}=24$ \si{\nano\second}, which is
reasonably close to the datasheet specification of 22 \si{\nano\second}. This delay
is relevant because the feedback loop relies on $Q$ updating in time, so at higher
clock speeds, too much delay could cause incorrect toggling.

    \centering

\end{figure}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:flipflop_feedback} shows a timing diagram we observed
for this setup where $Q$ output toggles on each rising edge of the clock to

\subsubsection{Cascaded flip-flops}

Next, we built a cascaded flip-flop circuit with feedback by connecting the
$\overline{Q}$ output to its own input $D$ to create toggle behavior. We drove
the clock input of the first flip-flop (U1) with a function generator and
clocked the second flip-flop (U2) using the $Q$ output of U1.\\

\noindent We observed the $Q$ output of U2 and found that it toggled at one-quarter of
the frequency of the original clock signal. Our setup functioned as a ripple
counter where U1 divided the input frequency by 2 and U2 divided it again by 
2. U2 changed state only when the output of U1 transitioned, which confirmed
the expected asynchronous behavior of a mod-4 counter. A sketch of our scope 
observations is included in Figure~\ref{fig:mod4_counter}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{10.2c.1.png}
    \caption{Timing diagram of the mod-4 ripple counter. Yellow is the clock input to U1, blue is the Q output of U2, and pink is what we expect for the output Q of U1 that feeds into the clock of U2.}
    \label{fig:mod4_counter}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Shift register}

Finally, we connected four D-type flip-flops in series to form a shift register
with the data input (IN) connected to the D input of U1 and all flip-flops
clocked in parallel using a function generator. We used an oscilloscope to
observe both the IN signal and the Q output of U1. There was a small "jitter"
(timing offset) between these two signals due to IN being stable before the
rising edge of the clock whereas Q1 updates slightly after it. This offset
is a result of the propagation delay of the flip-flop.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{10.2d.1.png}
    \caption{Timing diagram for data movement through the 4-bit shift register.}
    \label{fig:shift_register}
\end{figure}

\noindent We observed the Q outputs of U2, U3, and U4 along with IN. With each
rising edge of the clock, we saw the data shift sequentially through the
register from Q1 to Q2 to Q3 to Q4 (one bit per cycle). Thus, we confirm the
function of our shift-register circuit. Our observations are illustrated in
the timing diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:shift_register}.

\noindent We tested several PID parameter combinations and observed their

\begin{itemize}
