charging delay from the pull-up resistor, which limits the speed of rising transitions.

\noindent At low frequency, the inverter behaved as expected with symmetrical rise and 
fall times of around $t_\text{rise}=26.62\si{\micro\second}$ and $t_\text{fall}=25.60\si{\micro\second}$,
respectively. At high frequency, both transitions remained relatively sharp, with
significantly faster rise and fall times of $t_\text{rise}=177.1\si{\nano\second}$
and $t_\text{fall}=199.9\si{\nano\second}$, respectively.\\

\noindent Unlike the passive pull-up configuration, the rising edge of the
output does not break down at high frequencies since the PMOS transistor
actively sources current to drive the output high while the NMOS pulls it low.
In contrast, the passive pull-up relies on a resistor, which results in slower
RC-limited charging. This improvement in switching speed explains why nearly all
logic circuits use active pull-up design.

\subsection{D-type flip-flop}


\subsubsection{D-type flip-flop operation}

We tested the functionality of the D-type flip-flop by connecting a slide 
switch to the D input and using a debounced pushbutton switch to provide the
clock signal. As expected, the output Q updated to match the value of D only
on the rising edge of the clock, so we confirm that the flip-flop is positive-
edge triggered.\\

\noindent We also teseted the asynchronous inputs. Pulling the SET input low
immediately forced Q high regardless of the clock or D input. Similarly, pulling
the RESET input low forced Q to low. Both inputs operated asynchronously and
overrode the normal clock behavior. After releasing, the flip-flop went back to
regular edge-triggered operation.
